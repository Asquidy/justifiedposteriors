10:30Robin: The most clear thing we can say is that when we have a prediction market and some estimate we're trying to get, we're asking people to back it with capital. We say, "You have some insight. We've got a place to buy insight, but we don't buy insight from you unless you back your insight with capital." That's how a prediction market works. You can't just have a story. If you don't have capital, you have to partner with somebody with capital, because you have to make a bet in the market using your capital, and then if you're right, you get the payoff from your information. So that's a key feature of these markets: they require access to capital.

Now, you can have pretty thin markets with low capital and still be pretty informative. That's great. But if we want you to put a fair bit of work into it, then we need to pay you a lot, and that needs a thick market. And the thick market means you'll have to have a lot of capital to go up. So the more we're paying you for your information, the more access to capital you need to back that. Now, if it's a very short-term topic, you don't need to tie the capital up for very long. You just need access to maybe a lot of capital, but only for short periods, and you could repeatedly use that capital every day on a new topic and do 365 different topics in a year with the same capital, if they're all one-day topics. But the longer you need to hold onto your assets to get your payoff, the longer you have to put that capital in, and the opportunity cost of capital becomes more of a constraint on your participation. So that's the basic fact about these things: it's just cheaper to elicit information about short-term topics relative to long-term ones, because the long-term ones require people to tie up more capital over the longer run. It doesn't mean you can't do longer-term topics. It just means you'll have to pony up more payment to do that.

12:20It's also a matter that you don't necessarily need people to invest in the long run for a long-term topic. Most of the stock market is implicitly a long-term thing, but most people trading in the stock market are making relatively short-term trades. So what it's really about is: how long does your information last? Every piece of information has a certain timescale on which somebody else is gonna find out. If you wait that long to trade, you're risking somebody else trading on the information before you. So you need to trade on it before they do. It's also generally true that if you have a piece of information, you make more money on it by spreading the trade out over the maximum period you can. The shorter a time period you try to trade on, the less money you make. That's just a basic fact of the way financial markets work. So you wanna spread it out up until the point where somebody else is gonna learn that information. All information has a scale like that, and that's a key metric for trading. It's not about how long the fundamental resolution takes to happen. It's about how long each piece of information lasts.

For example, some people have high-frequency trading information. They just get fast information about some markets and trade it in other markets. That information is really short duration, so it's very profitable with respect to price, but the time period is so short that they can't spread their trades out. They have to make their trades really fast, so they can't make as much money on those trades. And it's not so clear that the rest of us really care about them profiting from that. But when we're designing these things, we wanna ask what kind of information we really want, and set up the system to encourage that kind of information. So again, to your question about long-term topics: it's not so much about the long-term topic. It's about the typical duration of the information someone could have on that topic. If you could have short-term information on a long-term topic, you can get people to reveal it with relatively cheap incentives, because they're gonna buy today, sell tomorrow to somebody else who buys and sells, and eventually the information gets out. Each trader didn't have to hold it very long.

Decision Markets and Why Manipulators Are Sheep 20:15

20:15Andrey: And where are we starting to see that?

Robin: Well, glad you asked. There's this company called MetaDAO in crypto, which creates what I call decision markets for governance. Many crypto companies, really all of them, have a token, and the token has a price in a market. Then a key governance decision comes before the organization, and the way to advise and even oversee that decision is a yes-or-no proposal. We make a token price if yes and a token price if no, and we approve the proposal if the first price is higher than the second, but not otherwise. This is what I call a decision market. MetaDAO made its constitution say it has to make its key decisions this way, and it's now had twenty-five-plus other firms who have incorporated under this sort of constitution, collectively making one hundred and fifty decisions this way. So they are now in the practice of using decision markets to make key governance choices in these crypto firms. That's the demand for information. These companies say, "We can't just rug-pull you. We can't just do whatever we want with the money. For key decisions, we need to get market approval through this mechanism." And that's how these companies are subsidizing these markets, so that the traders will give them information for making key governance choices.

Andrey: Just so I understand a little more clearly: let's say I wanna participate in this market. I'm gonna buy a bunch of tokens.

Robin: You could buy or sell, depending on which side you wanna go on.

Andrey: But let's say I want the decision to be made. I'm gonna buy a bunch of—

Robin: You want yes, okay? You could do that two ways. You could either push up the yes price or push down the no price. To push up the yes price, you just need cash: you go into the yes market and you buy tokens for cash. 22:18Andrey: The obvious thought here is that if I'm a whale, I could just buy the decision.

Robin: You think you could, but you can't.

Andrey: And what is the key thing that's preventing that?

Robin: All information institutions, including prediction markets and journalism and gossip and academic journals, and even podcasts, have a number of ways they could go wrong, or worries people have, and one of those is manipulation: people distort the output by pushing in distorted inputs. And that is a common problem in podcasts and journalism—

Andrey: No. Never. Not for us.

Robin: Not on this podcast. And gossip. But it's much less of a problem in prediction markets. This is one of the ways in which prediction markets excel compared to other information institutions. It turns out to be very hard to manipulate them.

So here's the simple theory. A simple model of financial markets, including prediction markets, is that there are two kinds of traders. I will call them sheep and wolves. The wolves are traders who have information, and their intent is to trade in the market on their information and profit from their trades. To do that, they need to trade against a sheep. If two wolves trade against each other, they've got opposite information, and on average that cancels. But if a wolf trades against a sheep, they make money, because a sheep is defined as somebody who doesn't have information. They're trading for any reason other than their information. And there are many reasons to trade. You could just be an idiot. You could have hedging needs, whatever it is. So sheep are traders who trade for reasons other than having information. Wolves are ones who trade on information.

The basic fact about markets is that sheep show up for whatever reason, and then wolves are attracted to the sheep. Wolves wanna go where the sheep are, 'cause that's how they make their profit. So the more sheep, the more wolves, and the harder wolves work to learn information and trade against the sheep. This is why, for example, a market on Google has more accurate prices than a penny stock: there are more sheep trading Google. Same for presidential markets being more accurate than a senatorial or mayoral race, because there are more sheep in the presidential market. So there are more wolves, more accuracy.

What does this have to do with manipulation? Manipulators are sheep. That's all you have to know. Manipulators are there for a reason other than the information they have, which means wolves would like to trade against them. And the more wolves expect manipulators to show up, the more they're gonna wanna show up on the opposite side of their trades, because they want to trade against sheep, and manipulators are sheep. This is not just some cute little story. We have game theory and math models that work this out. We have lab experiments. We have field data. This is actually how it works in the world. In markets, the wolves are attracted to the sheep.

25:07Now, there's actually a stark lesson here. Most financial markets work this way. The main reason most financial markets have the accuracy they do is that the wolves are feeding off the sheep. They all work because there are all these sheep willing to lose. Many of those are people who need to make trades. If you're saving for retirement, you have to put money in the stock market every year, and later you have to take it out in order to retire. Those are trades you can't escape having to make, even though you don't have information. But most of the traders in the markets are actually not those people. They're speculators. They're losers. They're fools, even. And most financial markets are basically run by taxing those fools. That's where most of the accuracy and most of the liquidity comes from: we tax the fools willing to show up in these markets, and that's also how prediction markets work. In some sense, we as a society have chosen that route to finance these markets. We could just directly subsidize these markets to get the information, if we were willing to pony that up. Instead we go, "Hey, these fools are showing up willing to be taxed. Fine, let's tax them." That's what we're doing.

26:11Seth: This is maybe a small tangent, but I can think of a third animal in this taxonomy, which maybe would be the ape. Robin: That's rare, but sometimes there are such strategies. There's enough competition for that role that you don't have any exclusive access to it. Lots of people are thinking all the time, "Could I do the squeeze thing? Am I gonna profit?" And most of the time they fail. Sometimes they succeed. On average, there's no exceptional profit from trying those strategies.

Robin: Yeah.

AI Forecasters and Prediction Markets 27:20

27:20Andrey: So there's been a lot of hype recently over these AI forecasting systems, and I'm curious how you think they will affect the feasibility of prediction markets. Are they a substitute? Are they a complement?

Robin: The idea of an institution in economics is a structure that's pretty robust to the details of the problem it's tackling or the participants involved. You want a structure that will, in general, produce the kind of outcomes you want in a wide range of particular circumstances. That's what an institution is. A prediction market is intended to be such an institution. In particular, it's supposed to be a forum where different kinds of participants can use different methods and different sources of information, and the mechanism itself doesn't have to care. It just has this general approach of rewarding the ones who are accurate and punishing the ones who aren't, and that allows it to be agnostic about the race or the gender or the age of participants, or whether they're human or AI. As long as we have an API that machines can use, which we've had for decades now, because most financial market trading has been done by machines for decades, then we don't really have to care who's participating.

But if a new source of participants shows up that's actually more cost-effective, better able to sit in that role, the thing you'd expect to see is that it becomes cheaper to use this as a way to buy information. I've said what I want in the future is somebody who says, "I want to know about my decision here. I'm willing to subsidize markets on my decision to get this information." But you'll have to pay a cost, and that'll be the cost of the information providers. How much trouble is it for them to collect the information, aggregate it, and then trade on it? If a new population of traders shows up who is just better at that, you'll find that you don't have to pay as much to get the information you wanted. That's the hope you can have with AI: that they will just show up as a new, better participant who sits in the slot. We don't have to change the slot itself. We could just get better participants.

I still have to say that's mostly a hope so far, in the sense that financial market trading is one of the areas where, for a long time, a lot of people have consistently said, "Is AI good enough for that now?" And they throw AI at it, decade after decade, and I don't think they're getting that much traction even now. But that's certainly a reasonable hope.

Cultural Drift: "We Broke It" 42:59

42:59Seth: One topic you've been working on and blogging about more recently is this idea of cultural drift, or cultural maladaptation. Can you briefly remind the audience what you mean by cultural drift, and why it's bad?

Robin: As you know, I've been a pretty optimistic technologist and futurist for a long time, just projecting previous economic trends. And I said, "Why are all these cultural trends dumping on fertility?" So I switched to studying culture and figuring out how it works. And there'll be hell to pay for breaking it.

44:03This will need a little bit of explaining. First, I wanna make the distinction between "of" and "in." Let's think about DNA evolution as an analogy. If we think about species, there are some habitats that are fragmented and small, like rivers and rainforests and coral reefs, and then there are some large habitats, like chunks of the ocean. The large habitats have large species, and the small habitats have small species. In a large species, evolution in the species goes faster. There are more places for an innovation to show up, and then it can spread to the whole species. In a tiny species, evolution in the species is gonna be slower. But then there's evolution of the features that a species has in common. All the members of a species have to have enough shared mating structure that when they mate, new things come out that work. Those features are defined by the species, and to have evolution of those, you need many species. So we have better evolution of species in fragmented habitats like coral reefs, and better evolution in species in the oceans. And when we look back and ask, "Where did most life today come from?" more of it came from the fragmented habitats, suggesting evolution of species actually mattered more than evolution in species, which might be surprising.

45:12Okay, so that's an analogy. Now, cultural evolution is a lot like DNA evolution, but it's faster and better. It's also crude, because we're pioneering it. Humans started this, and it wasn't happening much before. With DNA evolution, you have two parents, the people who handed you your DNA. In culture, you have as many parents as there are people whose behavior you model your behavior on, people whose behavior you saw and are now being like. And you have as many children as the people whose behavior you influence. This influence through parents and children can happen fast. It doesn't have to wait until someone is born to do the next iteration, and it can spread fast around the world and jump very far. So cultural evolution is a powerful thing.

It also has "of" and "in." A culture is a group of people who share a bunch of norms, status markers, and values, such that if you were to visibly deviate from them in that culture, you would be punished. Just like with a species, the members of a culture have to have some things in common to function. So there's evolution in cultures and evolution of cultures. A few big cultures would make for great evolution in the culture of the things you're allowed to vary. But to have evolution of cultures, you need many cultures. That's the key problem.

46:37Now, natural selection, Darwinian evolution: we like to think of it as an innovation process, making things better. But its first task is to prevent stuff from getting worse. Its first task is to be a control process. And natural selection has parameters such that, if they go bad enough, it stops working, things get bad, and control fails. Fortunately that isn't the usual case, but it is a common case. Natural selection can just break and go bad.

So if we think about the evolution of cultures, I want you to think of this mathematical model of natural selection in general. There's a high-dimensional space with an adaptive region in it, a good place. And there's a cloud of points that need to track this adaptive region. The parameters of the process are: first, how many points there are. Second, when a point's in the middle of the region, it grows and splits, and outside the region, it shrinks and dies. That's the strength of selection pressure: the points need to feel a difference, and have a different effect, inside versus outside. Third, how fast the region moves out from under all the points. And fourth, how much the points wiggle wildly, independent of everything else. You can see that at one extreme, it'll do fine. The cloud will track the region. If we move all four of these parameters to the other extreme, it'll do badly: the cloud will fail to track the region, and the points will all just die. That's what happens in natural selection. I'm gonna apply that now to the evolution of cultures.

48:09Think about the last few centuries of cultural evolution. Three centuries ago, we had hundreds of thousands of peasant cultures around the globe, each relatively independent of its neighbors, basically running its own world. These peasant cultures were near the edge of survival. They faced strong selection pressures: famines, pandemics, and wars that would kill them off if they went bad. High variety, strong selection pressure. The world economy doubled roughly every thousand years, and that was the rate at which they needed to change to keep up with the changing context. Manageable. And they were very conservative, trying not to change. They didn't think they were changing. They thought they were staying the same. They did change, but not very perceptibly, so they weren't wiggling much.

Now, what's happened over the last three centuries? First, we merged peasant cultures into nations. We created national cultures out of peasant cultures, a great reduction in variety, and then we merged national cultures into a global monoculture, an even further reduction in variety, especially among elites. So variety is way down. Second, these few remaining cultures are not much being killed off by famines or wars or pandemics. We're rich, we're healthy, we're peaceful, so there's not much selection pressure on them. Third, the world economy doubles every 20 years instead of every thousand, a factor-of-50 increase in the rate at which adaptation needs to happen, because the world's changing that much faster. And finally, instead of being conservative and trying not to change cultures, we decided to make cultural activism our highest honor and to celebrate the people who go out of their way to change our key norms and values, our culture. The other prediction is that because we have fewer, bigger cultures, we have better innovation in culture, and so we are in fact having faster technological and economic innovation. The things that are allowed to vary inside cultures are going great. But partly that's what's causing the rapid change that cultures are finding it hard to adapt to fast enough.
