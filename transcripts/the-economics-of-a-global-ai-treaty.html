This week we’re joined by Phil Trammell, economist at Stanford and senior researcher at Epoch AI, and co-author (with Chad Jones) of the “When GDP Misleads” paper we covered earlier. Phil is one of the most original thinkers on the economics of AI, and he brought his priors to an unusual assignment: a science-fiction scenario.

We read the AI 2040 “Plan A” essay: a future in which the US and China agree to pace and monitor AI development, data centers are placed where they’re easy to bomb (hello, Mongolia), and 2040 arrives with abundance, a citizen’s dividend, and lie detectors. Before reading, the three of us stated our priors on two questions: will an international AI pacing and transparency treaty exist by 2030, and would one with teeth be a good idea? Phil started at two-thirds and 75%; Seth at 30% and 60%; Andrey at 20% and 49%.

Along the way: whether the essay’s growth forecast is load-bearing (Phil: it’s close to a wash, and Seth and Andrey push back), why a world where AI looks like nuclear weapons might be easier to coordinate on, Chad Jones’s risk-aversion argument, whether AI itself makes monitoring and commitment cheaper, why monitoring every data center sounds absurd to Andrey and like scrubbers on smokestacks to Phil, mind-reading and privacy, and Seth’s worry that a one-time lump-sum rebate of the universe ends in an extreme power-law distribution of wealth. Then Phil delivers a mini-lecture on renegotiation-proofness, the piece of game theory he thinks the essay is missing, and on why it may be safer to go faster if most risk is state risk rather than transition risk.

We close with posteriors, a discussion of Phil’s work on recursive self-improvement (RSI), and a lightning round: why Epoch instead of Stanford or Anthropic, his giving pledge and wild animal welfare, and how a Piketty question from Dwarkesh turned into one of the most-read things Phil has written.
