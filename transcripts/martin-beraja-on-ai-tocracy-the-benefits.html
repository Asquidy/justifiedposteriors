This week, we’re joined by Martin Beraja of UC Berkeley Haas, one of the most prolific macroeconomists working on AI.

We start with his newest paper “Growth at the Resource Frontier” — with Noam Yuchtman — which argues that NYT v. OpenAI is just the latest instance of a very old pattern — innovators appropriate resources nobody had bothered to assign property rights to (oil, the electromagnetic spectrum, ad data), and the courts legitimate it afterward. Could it be that unclear property rights are actually a catalyst for innovation?

We then turn to Martin’s China work (including “AI-tocracy” — with Andrew Kao, David Yang, and Noam Yuchtman, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2023), where facial-recognition firms winning data-rich surveillance contracts went on to produce more commercial software, and where local unrest predicts AI procurement which then suppresses further unrest — a feedback loop that cuts against the standard “autocracy taxes innovation” story. Andrey pushes back on this hard.

Third: AI as a codification technology, successor to the scientific manual and the McDonald’s franchise binder — if new entrants were born with the organizational knowledge of forty-year-old firms, Martin’s new paper “The Value of Organizational Learning Technologies,” with Eduard Talamás, argues that US GDP would roughly double.

Lightning round covers saltwater versus freshwater economics, Bay Area agglomeration externalities, and why AI displacement may be less inefficient than robot displacement.

Now, the question that comes up: different from capital — which you don’t need to build yourself, you can rent it in the market or buy it from a different firm — this idea of organizational capital or knowledge is something the firm needs to accumulate internally. It cannot just buy it from the market. That’s why it takes so many decades to build up that knowledge.

That said, we have had, in different eras, different technologies that can codify that knowledge and then diffuse it to the rest of the economy. Later on we had scientific management in factories. And later, actual management — the idea that management is a technology that can translate best practices across businesses.

And franchising. I love franchising as an example of a codification technology, because it’s literally that. McDonald’s took a long time to figure out exactly how to produce a Big Mac. But once they figured it out, they could write down the instructions for how you set up a new McDonald’s.
